On November 5, the U.S. midterm results and an AI-sector selloff landed on the same day. The Nasdaq fell 5.52%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped more than 12%, Nvidia lost 9.3%, and roughly $3.2 trillion in market value disappeared. On that same day, OpenAI shipped a cybersecurity model, Anthropic's independent evaluation reported that its capability growth had doubled, and Google cut video generation prices by another 30%. Products kept moving forward, but the political and power conditions underneath them were repriced.

## One: The election result directly rewrites what is buildable

The market attributed the fall to one direction: after Democrats won both chambers, the political cost of AI expansion rose. The Verge counted 43 of 44 local data-center moratorium proposals passed, and an NBC poll showed 76% of voters opposed building data centers in their communities. The report also said Republicans' earlier attempt to override local restrictions through state law may be ending; OpenAI and Anthropic did not respond, and both CEOs were said to be privately discussing constrained compute capacity.

Losses concentrated where capex is most exposed: Nvidia down 9.3%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down more than 12%, SanDisk down 22%, and Micron, Western Digital and AMD each down about 17%. The S&P tech sector had its worst day since March 2020. The fear was aimed at the return assumptions behind borrowing to build data centers and leasing them to model companies, not at any single company's earnings.

The boundary matters: blaming the crash entirely on the election is market commentary, not established causation. Weaker Qualcomm guidance, memory prices and high rates weighing on long-term capex were also in play. The Chinese-language accounts citing a "2026 ban on artificial superintelligence" bill and a proposal to pause advanced AI development are single-source retellings, and neither proposal is law.

## Two: Capital did not stop: a 910 MW, roughly $28 billion lease

The same day, Riot Platforms disclosed in its quarterly filing an AI compute lease with Anthropic covering all 910 MW of IT capacity at its Corsicana, Texas campus, with a total contract value of about $28 billion. First delivery is expected in September 2027; the tenant will own and operate its own computing equipment, while Riot supplies land and power infrastructure. Capital expenditure is to be financed mainly through lease-backed debt that has not yet been arranged.

Read together with the selloff, the picture is complete. Public markets are discounting AI capex while private, decade-length contracts are still being signed. It also shows where risk sits: land, power and financing pressure stay on the data-center side, while equipment and technology-iteration risk sit with the tenant. Because the debt is not yet secured, whether the money arrives depends on credit markets rather than the share price.

The disclosure is a retelling of a quarterly filing; the counterparty has not publicly confirmed it, and the delivery date covers only the first phase.

## Three: GPT-6 Cyber ships a security model behind access tiers

OpenAI released GPT-6 Cyber, a cybersecurity model trained to find, validate and fix exploitable vulnerabilities, available through the Daybreak platform to vetted security teams. Greg Brockman said it is already helping defend security-critical software including the Linux kernel and Firefox, and that training focused on keeping the model within the scope the operator authorizes. Access splits into two tiers: Daybreak Red for offensive security testing and critical-infrastructure defenders, and Blue for general defensive teams. He also teased a cybersecurity announcement for November 12.

What is worth recording is the distribution model. The most capable cybersecurity model is not sold openly; it is released in tiers based on the defender's credentials. That contrasts with a16z's argument, published the same day, that exploit costs have fallen from weeks to hours. Offense keeps getting cheaper while the strongest defensive tooling acquires a gate. Whether that gate reaches the long tail that actually needs it — schools, hospitals, aging industrial systems — is the open question.

Boundary: the benchmark scores and field results come mainly from OpenAI's own materials, with no third-party replication yet. "Already defending the Linux kernel" is a company statement and does not mean upstream has adopted its patches.

## Four: METR finds capability growth doubled, with the best evidence redacted

The AI safety evaluator METR published a report on November 5 estimating that, through October 2026, Anthropic improved model capabilities on well-specified tasks about twice as fast as it did between March 2024 and October 2025, with a range of 1.3x to 3x. The most concrete metric is AECI growth at 1.9x the earlier rate (95% confidence interval 1.5x to 2.8x), with roughly 80% of the acceleration possibly attributable to Anthropic's heavy use of AI in its own R&D.

METR said the next 12 months could accelerate further, while acknowledging a limited vantage point. The report also noted a detail: Anthropic now keeps its newest models internal for longer before releasing them.

In the same report, the evidence chain matters more than the number. METR delivered a roughly 100-page report, and under their agreement Anthropic could unilaterally require removal of non-public information. What was removed included acceleration estimates for each training stage, estimates of algorithmic progress, the split between compute and data contributions, and predictions about future models. A planned audit of compute allocation was also dropped mid-project, before any substantive conclusion.

Anthropic co-founder Sam McCandlish responded that publishing this material would hand competitors an acceleration roadmap and amplify the very risks METR worries about. Two conclusions therefore hold at once: an outside evaluator believes acceleration is happening and that AI is the main driver, and the verifiability of outside evaluation is declining. More companies now cite independence as a safety argument, but what an independent evaluator can obtain and publish is still decided by the company being evaluated.

## Five: The Claude Code departures are about organization, not pay

Four core members of the Claude Code team left within a week, including product lead Cat Wu and desktop lead Anthony Morris, plus core engineering and engineering-management roles. Thariq Shihipar, one of the product's most visible engineers on X, had already left and appeared on a podcast. Several retellings say Cat Wu went to OpenAI.

The most widely shared version frames the cause as a 30% raise plus equity, but the original author noted those figures were illustrative, not factual. What can be verified is narrower: the departures come just as Anthropic prepares for an IPO, and the departing employees' public comments centered on organizational conditions — a swelling team, enterprise customer demands squeezing product rhythm, and the idea that identity should not be tied to one company. This looks more like friction as a product organization shifts from small-team iteration to serving large accounts than a simple poaching story.

The direct impact on users is unclear. Claude Code remains one of Anthropic's most direct revenue products, and whether the departures change release cadence will only show in the next few versions.

## Six: Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash goes from 10 to 15 seconds and cuts price 30%

Google released Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash, available in the Gemini API and Gemini for business. It natively supports clips up to 15 seconds, previously 10, adds multi-turn conversational editing and stronger reference-image consistency, and cuts 720p pricing by 30% to $0.07 per second.

Those parameters point at workflows rather than demos. Fifteen seconds covers a single shot in a short video; multi-turn editing turns "rewrite the prompt and regenerate" into local edits on an existing clip; at seven cents a second, a 15-second clip costs about a dollar, so teams can afford many more attempts. Most competing products in this tier still stop at 5 to 10 seconds and charge per clip.

Consistency and controllability remain vendor claims without third-party comparison, and commercial terms follow each platform's own rules.

## Seven: Two Chinese paths: Hunyuan Hy4 competes on price, Baidu opens its base model

Tencent Hunyuan's Hy4 scored 35 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index v5, second among Chinese lab models behind Kimi K3.1 Pro at 46, level with Grok 4.7 (xhigh) and one point below Claude Haiku 5.5 (max). On components, it scored 31% on FrontierCode 2.0, the highest among Chinese lab models, and 50% on AA-Omniscience v1.1 while showing an 89% hallucination rate. Tencent Technology reported that Hy4 uses a 770B mixture-of-experts architecture, that weights are planned for release soon, and that the team acknowledges a substantial gap to top closed models. Pricing is $0.834 per million input tokens and $2.501 per million output, with a 1M-token context window and text-plus-image input. A Hunyuan-relayed Agent Arena leaderboard places it 14th overall at $0.55 per task, about 62% below Kimi K3.1 Pro Max at $1.46, with less than one point separating their scores.

Baidu took the other path, open-sourcing the ERNIE 5.5 Flash Base foundation model: 280 billion total parameters with 14 billion active per token in a MoE architecture, pretrained from scratch on 33.5 trillion tokens, a 128K pretraining context, and 1M tokens for fine-tuning and inference. The company claims leadership on 30 benchmarks among base models of the same size, compared against DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Base and MiMo-V2.5-Base.

The trade-offs are explicit. Hunyuan buys ranking position with low price and accepts an 89% hallucination rate, which suits batch tasks that tolerate errors; Baidu bets on downstream fine-tuning and deployment ecosystems. "Best in class at the same size" is the publisher's chosen comparison set, and a high score on one dimension does not mean the model is production-ready.

## Eight: Google measures hypertension with a smartwatch against a clinical gold standard

Google Research published work in Nature using photoplethysmography (PPG) and accelerometer (ACC) signals from a consumer smartwatch to passively identify hypertension without cuff calibration. The method runs in two stages: self-supervised contrastive pretraining on 492,347 hours of unlabeled data from 2,109 participants to learn representations of 15-second waveforms, then person-level averaging across days plus height, fed into a regularized logistic regression that outputs a continuous risk score.

Validation was a prospective, multi-center study across five U.S. sites with 196 participants free of known cardiovascular disease and not taking blood-pressure medication, benchmarked against 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring. For initial screening it reached 65.8% sensitivity, 90.0% specificity and 80.6% positive predictive value, versus 55.3% sensitivity for office screening. Among the 48 participants with elevated office readings, confirmatory testing reached 78.4% sensitivity, 90.9% specificity and 96.7% PPV, close to multi-day home monitoring (89.2%, 81.8%, 94.3%) but with lower sensitivity.

Its significance lies in reframing the problem from absolute blood-pressure estimation to identifying a persistent hypertensive state, which sidesteps the calibration requirement for cuffless devices. But 196 participants and a single validation standard cannot support standalone screening claims; Google itself states this is early feasibility work that does not replace a cuff or represent a shipping product feature.

## Nine: Claude helps solve a vault structure, cutting sample prep from 3 days to 90 minutes

Anthropic's biological research team published results from using Claude in work on vaults, hollow protein barrels discovered in human cells in 1986. Nearly every cell contains them and they are among the largest known intracellular molecular structures, yet four decades on their function is unresolved: mice born without them live normally, and hypotheses about transport, toxin resistance and immunity remain unproven. A practical obstacle is that the standard model organisms — yeast, fruit flies, nematodes — lack vaults entirely.

Three results came with concrete numbers: the team showed for the first time that related proteins in bacteria and bacteriophages can also assemble into vaults; sample preparation dropped from about three days to 90 minutes; and solving one 3D structure took 34 hours for a first model. By increasing the repeating segments forming the wall from seven to nine, the team assembled particles with roughly 20% more internal volume, showing the barrel's size can be designed. Claude handled literature search, optimization of the particle-separation method and structural analysis, while humans performed the experiments.

The value of such cases is not that "AI discovered something" but that trial and error in experimental design gets compressed. The boundary is equally clear: structural results await peer review, the function question is unanswered, and the idea of turning vaults into enzyme-delivering nanocapsules remains distant.

## Ten: Agents start managing agents, and orchestration becomes the product

DeepSeek published a draft architecture for Super-agent: a Router hands work to parallel Workers that monitor GitHub discussions, investigate bugs and open pull requests, while a Watchdog checks who is stuck or drifting. In simulated tests, 95th-percentile task time fell from 29.5 to 15.7 minutes, at the cost of significantly higher token use; the tests did not measure code quality. The companion DeepSeek Harness prototype is open source, but Super-agent itself remains a proposal.

On the same day, Perplexity opened Teammates to Max subscribers, letting agents take on jobs proactively using Computer tasks, skills, memory and tools, collaborate with each other, and message colleagues in Slack. Aravind Srinivas said the internal workspace had about 10,000 teammates over 30 days, 20 times headcount, with roughly 1,000 active the previous day.

Notion's founder offered another data point: half of the pages on the platform are now created by agents, in-app AI usage grew ninefold this year, and third-party agent calls through MCP grew about 60-fold.

Together the three cases say the same thing: the bottleneck has moved from whether a model can do the work to how work is dispatched and accepted. That also explains why DeepSeek's test reports time but not quality — the first job of an orchestration layer is keeping parallelism from running away, and quality evaluation comes later.

Boundary: DeepSeek's numbers come from simulated tests, while Perplexity's and Notion's come from executives, with no third-party audit.

## Editorial conclusion

On a single day, products got cheaper, models got faster and contracts got signed, while the market repriced the entire compute chain. That is not contradictory: technology sets the ceiling, while power, land, credit and political permission determine how fast progress becomes capacity. What to watch is not another model score but two things — whether local data-center moratoriums spread from cases into a trend, and whether a redacted evaluation can still support the phrase "independently verified."
