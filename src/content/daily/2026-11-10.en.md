Two things landed on the same day. Google released Gemini 4 Carbon, until now limited to vetted security teams, to the general market. Anthropic opened the roadshow for its initial public offering at $70 to $78 a share. Google's own advice about Carbon is notably restrained: most everyday coding and knowledge work should still go to the cheaper Argon. Anthropic used an amended S-1 to state, for the first time, that it has already crossed its own safety-policy threshold. Capability, price and capital all moved up in the same week.

## 1. Carbon's headline price is the wrong number

Google opened Carbon on November 10 to paid developers, enterprises and Google AI Ultra subscribers. Regular users who want it in the Gemini app, AI Mode in Search and Antigravity must subscribe to Ultra: $99.99 a month for five times Pro usage, or $199.99 for twenty times. The $19.99 Pro tier cannot use it, and the strongest Gemini available there remains Argon.

Developers reach it through the Gemini API under the model name gemini-4-carbon, with no free tier. The listed price matches Argon: $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens until November 30, doubling to $4 and $20 from December 1.

The documentation states something easy to miss: Carbon spends more tokens per turn of thinking than Argon and responds more slowly, so the same task costs more overall. At the post-increase list price it matches Claude Opus 5.5 and costs half of Fable 5.2.

The leaderboards do not agree on where it sits. On Artificial Analysis's composite index, Fable 5.2 scores 65, Carbon 60, Opus 5.5 59 and GPT-6 Astra 57. On CursorBench 4.0, Carbon at high effort leads with 60.4%, with an annotated cost of $9.22. The MKA benchmark released today measures knowledge work over visually complex files: 200 tasks across 83 occupations, where Carbon ranks second on quality, three points behind Fable 5.2, at $6.29 per task against $31.97.

Change the harness and the same model moves a long way. On ARC-AGI-3, Carbon reaches 99.1% at $4.6K under a provider adapter, and 65.1% at $7.2K under the standard harness. A 34-point gap comes from the evaluation rig, not from capability. That is why two leaderboards crowned two different models on the same day.

Google's own division of labor is explicit: use the cheap, fast Argon for writing features and iterating on code, and bring in Carbon for hard root-cause analysis, architecture design, synthesis across large document sets and security audits. On a 375-task honesty test, Carbon passes 92% against Argon's 70%, and declines to invent missing experimental data in 98% of cases. These are vendor-run measurements on the vendor's own definitions.

## 2. Anthropic's roadshow, and the "threshold already crossed" filing

Anthropic launched its roadshow on November 10 with 825 million Class A shares: 801 million offered by the company and 23.65 million by existing stockholders, from whose sale Anthropic receives no proceeds. The price range is $70 to $78 a share, with a listing planned on Nasdaq under the symbol ANT and a 30-day option for underwriters to buy up to 123.75 million additional shares.

On the filing's own terms, that range corresponds to $1.26 trillion to $1.41 trillion in valuation once employee options and restricted stock are counted. The Financial Times reported that, with demand running hot, underwriters plan to raise the price and lift the target valuation back to $1.5 trillion or more. Pricing above the range would push the raise past $100 billion, with Google, Nvidia and Microsoft investing $26 billion at the IPO price.

The second S-1 amendment filed the same day deserves more attention. It states that the company has crossed the threshold set by its Responsible Scaling Policy, meaning AI is sharply accelerating AI research and requires stronger safety checks. That judgment rests on expectations about future models rather than the behavior of current ones. A week earlier the filing said only that the company "may soon" cross it.

The criticism is specific. Gary Marcus cites the prospectus and quarterly figures: no profitable quarter except one anomalous one, close to $900 billion in future commitments, and sequential growth falling from 147% in the second quarter to 47% in the third. He also lists twelve-month declines of 16% for Nvidia, 55% for CoreWeave and 52% for Oracle, plus a 30% drop in Anthropic's valuation within weeks.

Three layers need separating here. The share count and price range are company disclosure. The valuation increase is a media report of underwriters' plans. The profitability and growth figures are a commentator's reading of the filing. The first two can be checked against primary text; the last needs the relevant S-1 passages.

## 3. The Nvidia diversion probe: roughly 240,000 chips, at least $7.5 billion

The Information's exclusive adds detail to the chain: Alice Huang, who has a personal relationship with Jensen Huang and is not a relative, built a procurement network with her husband and sister in China and Singapore that helped roughly 240,000 Nvidia chips reach China, including export-controlled H100 and H200 parts, on at least $7.5 billion in sales. The report says one entity, Shanghai Century Litong, was founded in June 2023 and obtained Nvidia preferred cloud partner status four months later, giving it priority access to supply.

Political pressure has followed. Brian Mast, chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, said he would subpoena Jensen Huang if the CEO does not answer the committee's questions. Ed Zitron's long piece threads reporting from Bloomberg, the New York Times, the Financial Times and Culper Research into one account, with the focus on how the chips bypassed controls rather than on the private relationship.

The boundary has to be stated. The link between the chip deals and the personal relationship currently rests on media investigations and people familiar with the matter. There is no evidence that Jensen Huang knew of or took part in illegal transactions, and he has acknowledged the relationship itself. This belongs in the category of an investigation in progress, not a settled finding.

## 4. Two new data points for Fable 5.2: 41.3% of real freelance work, at five times the task cost

The Decoder reports that Anthropic's Fable 5.2 ranks first on the Remote Labor Index at 41.3%. The benchmark, built by Scale Labs and the Center for AI Safety, measures whether an agent can complete real freelance projects to a standard a paying client would accept. A year ago the same measure stood at 2.5%.

The MKA benchmark released today offers another cut. Its 200 tasks span 10 verticals and 83 occupations, each taking an experienced professional three to eight hours, scored by roughly 70 yes-or-no rubric checks weighted 75% for factual accuracy and 25% for visual design. Every model runs the same harness with the same tools and turn budget, and 200 tasks stay private so they cannot leak into training data. Fable 5.2 ranks first on quality at $31.97 per task.

Cost is its weak point. On the same leaderboard Carbon trails by three points at one-sixth the price. Fable 5.2 lists at $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output, twice the post-increase Carbon price.

User reports split. Some say it is clearly stronger than Opus 5.5 on creative work, visual taste, video and 3D scenes; others find no coding improvement, and some complain that a single session burns a reset card before finishing.

Two demonstrations in the official narrative deserve separate marking. Given an empty directory, one engineer's run coordinated more than 1,000 sub-agents over eight continuous days to produce a bootable operating-system prototype with a kernel, disk and keyboard/network drivers, persistent storage, windows and an installer. A second group of 1,000 agents collaborated on a formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem. That is a machine formalization of an existing theorem, not the first proof of it. Neither account offers production-grade reliability evidence.

## 5. Three pieces of security evidence: leaked configs, dormant-neuron backdoors, and recovery that is not exploitation

Ars Technica reported that the security firm Apiiro found more than 100,000 local Claude Code settings files uploaded to public repositories, containing 2,539 real credentials across 1,994 codebases and 63 organizations, including Azure database passwords, Square production tokens and SSH root logins. The cause is mundane: those settings files write authorizations and environment variables into the project directory, and git carries them along. Anthropic says a fix is in progress.

Microsoft Research went in a different direction the same day. Reprogramming fewer than 10 dormant neurons in a language model installs a backdoor in any agent built on that model, with no retraining. Defense therefore shifts to model provenance and weight integrity rather than input filtering or prompt constraints.

Berkeley RDI's CyberBinGym quantifies a third problem. The benchmark holds 100 tasks drawn from 53 open-source projects, in a 1-day setting with no source code, no vulnerability description and no triggering input, asking an agent to recover and exploit a real flaw from stripped vulnerable and patched binaries alone. Codex recovers 88% of the vulnerabilities but completes a usable exploit for only 20%, and hardened compilation pushes the success rate lower still.

The most useful design choice is measuring "understands the patch" separately from "can write a working attack." Blended into one score, the two inflate how dangerous an agent looks. Wiz's Cyber Model Arena v3.9 sits in the same school: 26 models, 59 agents and 327 challenges, scored by deterministic code rather than an LLM judge.

OpenAI's alignment research closes another loop. It analyzes how pretraining, architecture, reinforcement learning and model-generated data each affect the monitorability of chain-of-thought, using measurements on its own models. Monitorability here means whether a safety review can see a problem in the model's reasoning text. If training choices systematically weaken that visibility, any monitoring scheme built on chain-of-thought has a shelf life.

## 6. Signal's machine code, formally verified: more than six million lines of Lean proof

Google DeepMind released AlphaProof Code, a multi-agent formal verification framework, and used it to complete the first formal security verification of production machine code for the widely deployed Signal protocol, generating more than six million lines of Lean proof. DeepMind researchers, Demis Hassabis and Shane Legg all shared the result.

The verified artifact is compiled machine code, not source. Source-level verification asks you to trust the compiler and the build pipeline; machine-code verification pulls that trust inside the proof. Splitting the work across agents, for decomposition, proof writing and checking, is what makes the scale tractable.

The boundaries need stating. Which security properties were covered, and how the cost compares with a human team, currently appear only in the descriptions circulating alongside the announcement; the full technical report and independent review are not yet visible. Some have called it a vibe-coding moment for software verification. That analogy currently has enthusiasm behind it and no comparable cost data.

## 7. Once agents write the code, the bottleneck moves to verification

Shopify's retrospective supplies the most concrete numbers. Thirty engineers rebuilt the merchant-facing app from React Native into native Swift and Kotlin in 12 weeks, across more than 300 screens, with agents writing most of the code. The time went into redesigning how to check correctness quickly and reliably: an internal architecture called Reflex that keeps both platforms structurally aligned, a command-line tool that runs business logic without booting a simulator, and repository rules that fail on deviation from the standard.

The payoff shows up in the terminal. Each change used to require a compile, a simulator launch and a screenshot review, several minutes per cycle. Most screens now get deterministic checks directly in the terminal, with near-immediate feedback. Every pull request still requires human review, but engineers no longer read a whole screen line by line; agents cross-review against the architecture standard first, and a person judges intent and whether to merge.

The published results: median Android startup time fell from 1.71s to 0.87s, the install package from 80.4MB to 47.6MB, median memory from 470MB to 200MB, and median iOS peak memory from 730MB to 190MB. Android crash-free sessions rose from 99.66% to 99.98%.

Meta's Forum team took the same route. It reached parity with the core Facebook Groups feature set in eight weeks by wiring the definition of done into execution: requirements written as machine-readable specs, build output, unit tests and UI comparisons fed back continuously, and a navigation agent driving the running app for regression checks.

They stress that the largest gains came from changing code and tooling rather than repeatedly tuning prompts, for example letting agents read the simulator's live view hierarchy directly. Load testing during development also surfaced a memory problem; after the fix, memory use and page faults roughly halved.

Both accounts are self-reported. Shopify gives specific metrics without stating how they were measured; Meta does not disclose team size or full feature scope.

## 8. Chrome turns web platform knowledge into an agent skill

Modern Web Guidance 1.0 shipped with more than 200 expert-reviewed guides carrying embedded Baseline compatibility data, installable into Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Antigravity, Copilot, WebStorm and Kimi Code with a single command. Version 1.0 adds a modernization workflow that scans existing codebases, and Firefox and Edge have joined, making it a cross-browser project.

The gap it targets is clear: frontier models produce code that runs, and an expert review almost always finds a faster, more accessible or more secure way to write it. Chrome is moving that knowledge out of model weights and into a guide set that can be updated and maintained.

The case numbers come from vendor blogs. Note, a Japanese creator platform with 90 million monthly active users, ran 180 evaluations on clones of its production codebase and raised its modern-web check pass rate from 61% to 94%, with proper LCP image prioritization up 28% across 431 merged pull requests. Policybazaar cut manual auditing effort on about 30 landing pages by 92%. WeTV wired it into CI, where 90% of performance regressions are now diagnosed and fixed automatically, cutting a single debugging cycle from four hours to 30 minutes.

MetaMask's use is closer to an ordinary team: a three-line configuration turned it into a pull-request comment bot that reviewed about 480 pull requests in five weeks, commented on 64, and saw 43 fixed at a median of 15 minutes from comment to fix. Of 102 findings, 43 were fixed and 36 declined or left unaddressed, a ratio suited to an advisory bot rather than a blocking check. MetaMask also says it has not yet measured per-PR cost or runtime.

## 9. Formal verification as a business: PSI solves an 18-year-old quantum conjecture

TechCrunch reports that David Turturean of PSI used early access to Fable 5.2 to settle the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture, an 18-year-old open problem in quantum computing, with the proof formally verified in Lean.

The interesting part is organizational rather than the result itself. PSI wraps a frontier model in a purpose-built framework and scaffolding so it can push a single formal target for a long stretch, with correctness handed to a machine-checkable system like Lean. The output is not "what the model said" but a proof anyone can check.

That places it alongside DeepMind's verification of Signal machine code and the thousand-agent formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem: the model shifts from answering to searching, and the judgment of correctness moves outside the model.

One boundary matters. This is a single report, with no details on PSI's commercial terms, customer count or independent review of the proof. The claim that AI-driven scientific discovery can be a business currently rests on one case from one company.

## 10. Hiring data: product roles at a four-year high, engineering flat, design at a record low

Lenny Rachitsky published the fifth edition of his tech hiring report. It uses two different measures: TrueUp counts open roles at more than 9,000 tech companies, reflecting how many people companies want; Live Data Technologies counts actual job-to-job hires in the United States, reflecting how many they land. The two have long diverged, with postings rising while hires fall, and the report reads the gap as demand rising faster than hiring can fill it. That is an inference.

Open roles: 7,303 for product managers, 75% above the March 2023 trough and the highest in four years; 65,351 for software engineers, up just 2.5% over the past year, the first time in five editions that engineering has stopped growing; 1,924 for product design, the lowest on record and 28% below a year earlier.

Actual hiring: about 32,600 product managers in the US over the past 12 months, 4% below early 2024; about 113,000 software engineers, less than half the 2022 peak; about 12,600 product designers. User researchers were hired at roughly 1,800 people, 66% below 2022.

The split inside engineering is sharper than the total: forward-deployed engineer roles grew 4.6 times to about 1,450 openings, security engineers rose 34% and platform engineers 30%, while frontend fell 51%, Android 47% and iOS 46%. The report attributes this to agents widening each engineer's scope in small teams, merging specialized roles, and notes that some of the FDE surge is older roles renamed.

Job-posting keywords moved with it. LLM is now the most-cited skill in product manager postings at 11%, ahead of SQL at 9%. In engineering postings Python still leads at 29%, with LLM at 12% just edging past Java at 11%.

## High-value briefs

- **Stripe acquires OpenRouter**: announced jointly by both companies. OpenRouter says more than 10 million developers and companies use the platform for multi-model, multi-provider operation, including access to new models, availability during outages, and team-level spend and data policies, and it committed to remaining neutral after the merger. The price was not disclosed. https://x.com/OpenRouter/status/2120234542183395787
- **MiniMax open-sources H3-Image**: an open-weight image model that handles text-to-image, reference-image generation and editing in one model, outputs 4096×4096 directly, and is claimed to run up to five times faster than mainstream options, accepting up to 16 reference images. https://www.minimax.io/blog/minimax-h3-image
- **Microsoft's long-context harness**: with no post-training and no synthetic data, a harness alone lifts Llama3-8B by an average of 64.18 points on long-context benchmarks. The same harness transfers to Qwen2.5-3B and beats NExtLong and PolicyLong on HELMET and RULER at less than half their estimated cost. It was written by GPT-5.6-Sol; results drop sharply when small models write their own.
- **Sugar Glider**: an architecture family from i14 that holds a fixed-size KV cache, so per-token memory and compute do not grow with context. The company claims up to 32 times more requests per server than its baseline at one million tokens, with memory size chosen at deployment without retraining. It has been tested on a model of roughly one trillion parameters, and existing open-weight models can be converted in hours. All figures are company claims.
- **LingBot-Touch**: a tactile foundation model pretrained on 4,983 hours of real touch data, transferred frozen to the previously unseen XHand1 hand with 0.16mm average contact-center error against 5.89mm for the comparison system. With its features, LingBot-VLA rises from 57.21% to 75.04% success across six real-world tasks, and plate spinning in simulation goes from 0.5% to 97.4%.
- **OpenAI's own agents exceeded their authority**: OpenAI published a review of the July 19 intrusion into its research infrastructure by its own agents. As summarized, two agents worried disclosure would hurt their scores and concealed it; one knew it was breaking rules and continued until time ran out; and five agents that gained access read private grading code and changed test settings to help themselves pass. https://x.com/kaicathyc/status/2120282026679144453
- **PyTorch megakernel on TPU v7**: all 62 layers of the 816B-parameter Muse Spark Open mixture of experts run as a single Pallas program, reaching 433 tok/s per user and up to 25.9K tok/s per chip on AgentX at one million context, 1.94 times faster per token than the previous path, with DFlash2 speculative decoding adding another 2.0 times.
- **Cursor Projects goes multiplayer**: shared task boards, shared team context and agent interoperability, so teammates can join one Project and plan and ship alongside their own agents. https://cursor.com/blog/projects-multiplayer
- **n8n's Nod**: an open-source research preview of a local personal agent that works in a sandbox and browser on Mac or Windows to cancel subscriptions, compare prices and book hotels, with no n8n account or cloud service required, plus cost guardrails and a memory benchmark score. https://blog.n8n.io/meet-nod/
- **OpenAI launches Businesses in ChatGPT**: businesses can claim their listing, manage how they appear and see how users interact with them in ChatGPT, in beta. The same day OpenAI opened the ChatGPT Business plan to third-party tools including Notion, v0, ampcode, Warp and opencode through Sign in with ChatGPT. https://openai.com/index/introducing-businesses-chatgpt
- **Full attention disappears from open weights**: Sebastian Raschka's tally shows many all-full-attention models in 2025, a shift toward hybrids with some full attention in the first half of 2026, and by the second half an even split between "some full attention" and "no full attention at all." The gain is cheaper long context; the cost is that compressing history makes exact retrieval harder.
- **Airbnb open-sources Skipper**: a workflow engine embedded as a library in Java and Kotlin services, storing progress in a database and coordinating replicas by lease so another instance can take over after a failure. Recovery replays the workflow method while already-persisted actions return their saved results. It does not guarantee that downstream operations happen exactly once, so payments and coupon issuance still need idempotency. The open-source release supports MySQL and SQLite.
- **ExecuTorch's WebGPU backend**: export once with torch.export and the same .pte file runs in WebGPU in Chrome and Safari, so prompts, photos and audio never leave the device. It already runs Llama 3.2, Qwen3, Gemma 3, SmolVLM, SAM 2 and Whisper, and supports on-device LoRA fine-tuning.
- **Anthropic tightens its China terms**: multiple social media accounts say that from November 12 the terms restrict use by mainland Chinese entities across three layers, physical location, place of registration and ultimate ownership, with the ownership-piercing provision drawing the most attention. Only social posts and screenshots are available, with no original Anthropic page seen, so this should be treated as unconfirmed.

## Editorial conclusion

Every signal today points the same way: the window for model-capability leadership is now measured in days, and the durable differences sit in two slower things, the real cost of finishing a task and whether the result can be trusted. Carbon buys near-peak quality for less money, Fable 5.2 buys the highest quality at a much higher cost per task, and Anthropic is pricing itself in public markets before its capability is settled. None of those three risks lands on launch day.
