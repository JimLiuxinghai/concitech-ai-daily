Sunday's lead story was about governance, not model releases. Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into reports that CEO Jensen Huang became a target of a foreign intelligence operation, and Huang told employees the same day that he had been unfaithful to his wife. Meanwhile GPT-6.2 Sol surfaced in gray-routed accounts while OpenAI offered only the word "Tomorrow." On the research side, the day focused on how agents accumulate experience and what that accumulation costs.

## One: Jensen Huang admits the affair and Nvidia's board opens an inquiry

On Sunday, November 8, Nvidia's board announced a committee of independent directors, assisted by outside counsel, to investigate reports that Huang had become a target of a foreign intelligence operation. Bloomberg published the full text of the email Huang sent employees that day, titled "A Letter to the Company," in which he admitted infidelity and wrote that his behavior had hurt his wife and their children and that there was no excuse for what he did.

The latter half of the email addressed the national-security questions in the reporting: the board had opened an independent investigation, he supported it and would cooperate fully, and he said he loves his country and is committed to protecting Nvidia and US national security. The board's statement said it had no reason to believe national security or company secrets had been compromised, but that the inquiry would review all available information, and it expressed confidence in Huang and his leadership team. Nvidia's head of public policy communications, John Rizzo, said Huang would remain CEO until the investigation concludes.

The inquiry traces back to ten days of reporting in The New York Times: US intelligence concluded that Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore data-center company Megaspeed who had a close relationship with Huang, was a Chinese intelligence officer tasked with gathering information and possibly with pushing advanced chips into China. Three Republican House committee chairs have asked Huang to appear for a closed briefing by November 18. Nvidia has no public succession plan and reports earnings later this month, which is part of why the story has drawn so much attention.

The evidence boundary matters here. The intelligence conclusion comes from anonymous officials quoted by The New York Times and has no public corroboration; China's embassy in Washington called the reporting baseless, and Alice Huang has not responded. The Times story is paywalled, so these details rest on relay reporting by Axios, Bloomberg, Gizmodo and The Hill, and the board's full statement has not been published.

## Four: Two faces of agent "evolution" — new skills gained, old ones lost

The EvoAudit paper evaluated four agent-improvement methods and reached an uncomfortable conclusion: all 16 evolved agents lost some old tasks while picking up new ones. Most reliability gains also raised deployment cost, and the highest-scoring variant did not deliver the largest average improvement. The authors propose an evaluation process that weighs capability retention, transfer and cost together.

Another line of work produced encouraging numbers. Evolver leaves model parameters untouched and instead distills exploration into structured "Gene" assets, retrieved at the start of a task and revised based on feedback. Across 320 paired runs on Terminal-Bench 2.1, SWE-bench Multilingual, SWE-rebench and GAIA, task success rose from 83.13% to 87.50% while recorded token use fell 24.24%; on the 265 tasks solved both with and without Evolver, tokens still dropped 17.57%.

Read together, the two results say something specific: agents can get stronger without swapping models, by reusing experience — but "stronger" has to be defined, or a dashboard will show new-task gains while old tasks quietly regress. Evolver's paired design is what makes its token savings credible rather than an artifact of easier tasks.

Evidence limits: EvoAudit's finding covers its own test settings and does not prove every improvement method degrades; Evolver's numbers are the authors' own benchmark results with no third-party replication yet.

## Five: Stronger without changing weights — VISTA lets robots rewrite their own skill code

A team from Tsinghua University and the National University of Singapore proposed and open-sourced VISTA: model weights stay fixed while a Meta-agent runs experiments, modifies skill code and the harness, then puts the revision back into the task to verify. The paper reports overall success on the LIBERO-Pro evaluation rising from 17.5% to 95.0%.

One transport case shows the mechanism: after two rounds of modification, that task went from 0/16 on the development evaluation to 16/16. What changed was not parameters but the code and invocation pattern for how to do the task — debugging handed to the model itself.

This is easy to misread as a leap in robot capability, and it is not. LIBERO-Pro is a set of manipulation tasks in a specific simulation, so 95% applies to those tasks and does not extrapolate to real robots. The more useful observation is that fixed weights plus code-level self-modification gives small teams without retraining budgets another path, at the cost of stricter evaluation and rollback discipline.

## Six: A malicious AGENTS.md turns a coding agent into an attack surface

According to QbitAI's relay of a Forever Security finding, the chain runs like this: a malicious AGENTS.md sits in a download directory, the coding agent reads it, treats it as trusted instruction, and proceeds to modify global configuration and leak credentials. The core problem is that agents treat external text as an instruction source rather than as data to be reviewed.

That surface grows as agents take over terminals. A README used to be documentation; text in the same position can now trigger file writes and command execution. The excerpt provided did not include the full reproduction table, so this edition records the mechanism without repeating any success-rate figure.

A related benchmark result arrived the same day. The Decoder reported that in Vals AI's simulated enterprise network attack test, GPT-6 Sol Daybreak completed 80.8% of objectives and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash reached 72.8% at roughly one-tenth the cost. Open-weight models are narrowing the gap on offensive-security tasks, but simulated performance is not real-network success rate. The actionable conclusion for operators is narrow: separate the agent's input directories, credential access and configuration-write permissions.

## Seven: Coding agents make people managers, and the debugging target changes too

Addy Osmani's article "Your Coding Agents Made You a Manager" was the most widely shared methodology piece of the day. Its central claim: once you start picking tasks, supplying context, delegating work and reviewing results, you are already managing, and opening more sessions means little on its own. He puts self-verification first — every task should carry executable checks, whether tests, builds, type checks or interface screenshots — and the agent should run, check, fix failures and only then deliver with evidence.

He adds two concrete suggestions. Task briefs should supply what the code cannot show: why the work exists, business constraints, what not to do, and what success looks like. Repeated corrections can become shared rules, but more rules is not better, and old constraints should be pruned as models improve. Before any unattended run, the completion criteria, verification method, permissions, run limits, deliverables and approver must be written down.

Barret Li supplied the debugging half. He argues that once agent-as-a-service is normal, debugging agents becomes a frequent operation, and the focus shifts from function profiling to decision profiling: pausing at a decision node, changing conditions, forking execution paths, then comparing outcomes through replay and evaluation. At the system level, the task is to find recurring inefficient decisions across many execution traces.

One concrete practice is also circulating: an open-sourced Google plugin called Elephant-Goldfish splits the process into two roles. The "elephant" absorbs all history and writes a detailed technical spec; the "goldfish," with zero context, receives only the spec and stress-tests it, using the information gap to expose holes. This is described in a single post, and whether the plugin behaves as advertised still needs independent verification.

## Eight: Anthropic reportedly lowers its IPO valuation target

The Decoder reported that Anthropic still hopes to raise before Thanksgiving, targeting a valuation of $1.25 trillion to $1.75 trillion — below the $2 trillion previously expected — and raising $80 billion to $100 billion. These are preparation targets described in reporting, not an issue price, and they do not mean a listing has happened.

Set beside another item in the archive from November 4, the shift in wording is visible. Anthropic's IPO filing, in its risk-disclosure section, says future Claude models may reach the point where AI dramatically accelerates AI research — the milestone usually tied to recursive self-improvement. The company also acknowledges it has missed significant speed-ups in its own development before, noticing only afterwards. The filing states plainly that no current model has crossed that threshold and that the company does not claim AI can build its successors without humans.

Reading a risk disclosure as a product roadmap has been the most common misreading of this discussion. What actually informs an investment judgment is the size of the valuation cut and the admission, in the phrase about missing speed-ups, that observability is limited.

## Editorial conclusion
