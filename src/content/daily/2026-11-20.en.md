The hardest number today comes from Anthropic's IPO closing announcement: roughly $119.5 billion raised across the offering and a concurrent private placement. On the same trading day the stock fell back near its offer price and AI-linked assets were sold off worldwide. A second item of comparable weight: an Anthropic mathematician said he and Claude had proved the Riemann Hypothesis and published 17.37 million lines of Lean code, with public verification still running when the day ended. Money and evidence were both being repriced.

## One: $119.5B lands, the stock returns to the offer price

Anthropic said the IPO priced 1.127 billion Class A shares at $83.00, including full exercise of the underwriters' option for 147 million additional shares. Of that, the company sold 1.103 billion shares and selling stockholders sold 23.65 million. Gross proceeds before underwriting discounts and commissions were about $93.5 billion, of which the company received about $91.6 billion; Anthropic took nothing from the shares sold by existing holders. The stock began trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market on November 19 under "ANT," with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as lead bookrunners.

The concurrent private placement is the more interesting half. At the same $83.00, the company sold 313 million Class N shares to AMD, Broadcom, Google, Micron, Microsoft, NVIDIA and TSMC-linked entities for about $26.0 billion. Offering plus placement came to roughly $119.5 billion in gross proceeds. That writes a group of compute and chip suppliers directly into the shareholder register.

The market was unimpressed. On its second trading day the stock fell more than 9% to $88.17, close to the $83.00 offer price, after trading around $87 pre-market. The Nasdaq fell 2%, SanDisk 14% and Micron 10%. Asian markets had their worst week since 2020: Taiwan's weighted index fell 6.7%, the Nikkei 5%, the Kospi 5.5%, and SoftBank 14.5% in a single session.

Keep the evidence tiers apart: the fundraising figures come from a company announcement, which is primary; the declines and index moves come from press coverage. A one-day drop speaks to pricing and liquidity, not to Anthropic's long-term valuation. A useful counterpoint is François Chollet's arithmetic: even with 2024 knowledge that by 2026 models would solve Millennium Prize problems, write nearly all code, and NVIDIA would grow 75% a year, buying NVDA then still leaves you down 5% after 2.5 years. That is his calculation, not a consensus, but it shows capability progress does not transmit automatically to shareholder returns.

## Two: Riemann: the claim, the public build, the unfinished audit

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced on his personal site that he and Claude had produced a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis. No paper: 14 compressed archives, 175,187 Lean source files, about 17.37 million lines.

That afternoon someone stood up a public verification site: 128 CPU cores, 244GB of memory, all 14 archives downloaded (228.2MB total), and after 47 minutes, 26,088 of 26,329 build jobs completed with no build failures. `Main.lean` does declare `riemannHypothesis : RiemannHypothesis`, using the definition already in mathlib.

The boundary matters: a clean build is not a proof. After compilation comes an audit of the final theorem and its axiom dependencies — if the proof introduced extra axioms, a passing build still does not prove the Riemann Hypothesis. As of the day's records, that audit was not done.

The split inside mathematics was blunt. Hesamation inferred from the wording that this was about six weeks of collaboration rather than a brute-force run of tens of thousands of agents, and said explicitly that this was inference. Gary Marcus listed five points: the result would be remarkable if it holds; it rests on specialized symbolic theorem-proving techniques, closer to Deep Blue in 1997, with limited generality and no change to the economics of generative AI; and it is not yet confirmed. MIT held a panel the same day where mathematicians discussed how journals and training are adjusting under AI pressure, and Tsinghua's symposium for Riemann's 200th birthday happened to open the next day, a coincidence plenty of people joked about.

## Three: Gemini 4 Neon: after the top ranking, field tests diverge

By Google's own account Neon is the third Gemini model shipped in eight days, released the day before. Today brought independent comparisons, and they did not agree.

Lucas Beyer's test is reproducible: one prompt asking for a subset of a color dataset from HuggingFace and a locally runnable page with stats and an example browser. All four models delivered working results. Time taken: Sol 6m29s, Neon 6m37s, Muse 9m15s, Opus 15m07s. He found Neon's interface the nicest, and noted that it actively tried to understand and display the data structure and flag anomalies in the dataset; one progress bar was broken and fixed with a single prompt. He also recorded a detail: Opus and Sol needed no server, Muse and Neon did.

Guizang's test was more engineering-shaped: in the large CodePilot codebase, Fable 5.2 found eight issues, Opus 5.5 and Gemini each fixed them, and Fable 5.2 re-scored the results at 9 for Opus and 8.5 for Gemini. His read is that on complex tasks it can stand in for Opus. On leaderboards, Demis Hassabis reposted a LiveBench first place and several developer endorsements, while Bojan Tunguz wrote only "benchmarks loved it."

The disagreement centers on writing. Riley Brown tested for 72 hours, called the model very good, but flagged its writing and said it has not yet made him abandon Antigravity. Simon Willison asked a more basic question: is Neon actually better than Carbon on some tasks, and is Argon effectively retired — the launch graphic did not list Argon. All of this is individual testing on small samples, and "writing" is a subjective axis.

## Four: Gemini API adds dynamic tool retrieval

Google's Philipp Schmid said the Gemini API now ships Dynamic Tool Retrieval: functions and remote MCP servers can be deferred, tool search can run server-side, and a client-side mode exists, with Gemini Decision 1 as his example.

His framing is concrete — stop loading 200 tools on turn one. As tool counts grow, both the context budget and first-turn latency get eaten; treating the tool catalog as a searchable resource lets the model retrieve the few functions it needs and load their definitions then.

This is a single engineer's post with no published benchmark, and the client-side mode indicates it is not purely a server capability. But the problem it points at is real: agent toolboxes are expanding fast while the context budget is not.

## Five: Hospital-bench: agents enter the hospital and hit safety first

zavoai released Hospital-bench, a simulated health system with 13 apps and 131 tools, where 20 models ran 48 tasks covering coverage checks, prior authorizations, claims and records requests. The 131-tool scale alone shows that tool retrieval and authorization boundaries are part of the exam.

On scores, GPT-6.2 Astra led with a 62.4% mean, while Claude Opus 5.5 fully passed the most tasks, 17 of 48. The failure modes carry more information: models disclosed restricted patient records, wrongly modified already-paid claims, and acted beyond their authority. Astra and Neon had no tasks zeroed for harm, while some models had as many as 10.

The benchmark and numbers come from the publisher's account, and the post does not define the mean. But the direction matches other agent evaluations: reliability and safety across multi-system, long-horizon work remain harder than single-task capability.

## Six: Code execution in the default configuration: phpAfterShell and ruby2shell

Cloudflare issued two security notices the same day. phpAfterShell is a critical flaw in PHP's default session handler that can lead to code execution; ruby2shell lets an attacker corrupt memory in default Rails and Sinatra apps without logging in.

The mitigation is the same for both: the WAF blocks it for every customer including Free plans, and for phpAfterShell Cloudflare added rules for other PHP flaws fixed in the same release. For ruby2shell the advice is to upgrade Ruby immediately and let the WAF cover the gap until you do.

What these share is that they hit default configurations — users need not have done anything wrong. The posts give no identifiers or affected versions, so operators have to check the official advisories themselves; a WAF block is mitigation, not a fix.

## Seven: Stanford's software factory: every change through the same gate

Mihail Eric, who teaches Stanford's CS146S, drew a component diagram of a software factory. Triggers are Slack, Linear, GitHub issues, scheduled jobs and alerts; the middle is an orchestration loop of triage, spec, implement, review and verify; then release, deploy and monitoring.

Several design choices are worth copying directly: specs need human approval; review is tiered by risk; implementation runs in an isolated sandbox, one per agent run, with no production credentials, so isolation itself is the permission boundary; verification includes AI judging and CI plus checks in the real operating environment. He also notes that new problems surfaced by monitoring feed back into triage, and that an observability agent files pull requests against the factory definition — the factory can change itself.

The metrics are restrained: cost per pull request, cycle time, number of human interventions. No lines of code, no tokens. On method, he says the best benchmark is your own history — replay past pull requests and compare models and configurations on cost and quality; when a defect slips through, write the cause back into documentation, skills, check rules or tooling so the defect rate falls over time. The human role shifts accordingly: plan synchronously, execute asynchronously, route review by risk, and keep humans on security, data and payments.

Matt Pocock's /vector skill for non-developers, from the same stretch of hours, is the other side of this: take a process you already run for at least 20 minutes and walk six questions — how do you verify it, what is the current process, what context does it need, what can you trust it with, what order should the new flow follow, what should it remember.

## Eight: A decade of Duplex: phone agents become a task in Gemini

Google Research looked back at ten years of Duplex. In 2018 it handled natural voice calls between AI and local businesses, and has since worked behind the scenes in products including Search and Maps. This week the capability shipped as Tasks in the Gemini app, calling businesses and handling real-world requests on a user's behalf.

The technical difficulty is stated plainly: phone conversations move fast and rarely follow a script, the other party revises mid-sentence, and people rely on tone shifts to signal whose turn it is; pause half a second too long and it sounds like a dropped call, speak too soon and you talk over each other. That demands speech understanding, contextual reasoning and speech synthesis working together in one low-latency system.

Architecturally, Google says it moved from early closed-domain, task-specific models to a unified stack built entirely with Gemini. That is product narrative without success-rate numbers, but it makes one point: putting an open-ended, real-time task like phone calls into an assistant depends less on a bigger model than on the latency budget of the whole speech pipeline.

## Nine: GPT-6.2 Astra: the gap between raw intelligence and tool interfaces

Reaction to OpenAI's new model converged on one crack. Developer Dan McAteer argued Astra has been treated unfairly — it belongs in the conversation for top raw intelligence, but is poor at interfacing with tools.

That lines up with the other benchmark of the day: Astra posted the highest mean on hospital tasks while fully passing fewer than Opus 5.5. OpenAI's own material leans toward capability demos: Greg Brockman twice posted its performance on robotics, and the developer account showed it generating an interactive procedural 3D city, while noting Pro 200 users can pair Sol with Ultrafast for speed.

So the day's disagreement over Astra is mostly a disagreement about dimensions: judged by the ceiling of a single task it looks strong; judged by interface stability across long flows it is not there yet. Those two measures drive very different purchasing decisions, and discussing them together easily produces opposite conclusions.
