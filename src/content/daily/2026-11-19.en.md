Two threads interlock today. Anthropic completed the largest IPO on record on Nasdaq — priced at $83, opening at $111.50, raising $81.3 billion — while Nvidia reported quarterly revenue that doubled year over year and closed at $145.98, a 52-week low. The market's pricing of AI has begun to separate from companies' current results. The second thread is control: Washington tightened both advanced-node foundry work and overseas compute access at once, forcing Chinese training plans to reroute. On the product side, Google moved a real-time world model and tool retrieval into its API, while OpenAI and Cline each offered a sample of treating agents as production systems.

## 1. Anthropic's IPO: two market-cap numbers, two rulers

The $83 offer price came in above the earlier $70–78 range. Within minutes of trading the stock reached $111.50, 33% above the offer; it then fell back, and a social-media tally put the first-day close at $97.17, up 17%. The offering raised $81.3 billion, surpassing SpaceX to become the largest IPO on record.

Two market-cap figures then circulated. TechCrunch said more than $2 trillion based on the opening price; The Verge put it at roughly $1.5 trillion on a fully diluted basis. Neither is wrong, but they use different rulers: the first multiplies an intraday price by shares, the second reflects the offer price. Better to state the basis than to argue over which is more accurate.

The ownership structure is the other half of the pricing. Amazon holds 14.7%, about $159 billion; Google holds 13.7%, about $149 billion; Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron and TSMC also took part in a $26 billion private placement at $83. The filing's other side is commitment: $900.9 billion in long-term compute and infrastructure purchases, $70.7 billion of it due within 12 months; annualized revenue run-rate of $81 billion as of late October, an implied 85% gross margin, and compute and infrastructure consuming nearly 60% of revenue. The FT's calculation is that a 20% return on roughly $1 trillion of compute commitments would require about $602 billion in annual revenue.

Control did not dilute with equity. Seven co-founders split the shares evenly, about $20 billion on paper each at the $83 offer price; together they hold 12.8% of the economics and 50.1% of the votes. Public markets bought cash flow, not decision rights. For a company that writes "safety" into its governance, that structure explains both why it could restate its willingness to slow training and deployment on IPO day, and why outside shareholders will struggle to change it.

The same day, an AI-safety circle produced the "MisAnthropic Statement," signed across safety advocates, open-source supporters and e/acc, accusing the company of advancing the very technology it warned about while using regulation to entrench itself. Dario Amodei's IPO statement stressed no capitulation to commercial or political pressure and a slower pace of training and deployment; he framed the mission through his biology background and Claude's use in research, acknowledged misuse, economic shock and loss of control as risks, and advocated racing "to the top." The petition expresses a policy disagreement, not a factual charge. The financial details come from a long social post rather than the prospectus itself, so that uncertainty stays attached.

## 2. Nvidia: revenue doubles, the stock bottoms in the same quarter

Third-quarter revenue was $115.844 billion, double a year earlier; net income $65.3 billion; data center revenue $107.6 billion, more than 90% of the total; the Vera Rubin platform has begun shipping and contributed nearly $20 billion. On the hardware side these are unusually strong numbers.

The stock took a different path. Hit by a broad chip selloff, the resignation of former CEO Jensen Huang and a governance investigation, the shares traded near $150 at one point, almost 40% below the October 6 high, and closed Thursday at $145.98, a 52-week low. An interim CEO is running the company while the board searches for a permanent successor.

Governance moves are multiplying. More than 10,000 employees, about 24% of the workforce, signed a petition asking for Huang's return; Bezos said publicly that outside professional managers rarely succeed at complex companies. Huang himself said he would be "very willing" to return as CEO. The board created a standing national security and global trade compliance committee chaired by independent director Suzanne Nora Johnson, meeting at least quarterly and retaining outside legal and trade experts, with compliance reporting both to the committee and the interim CEO.

The boundary is causation. The company has not explained the resignation; social posts linking "national security" to it remain speculation. The employee petition and Bezos's remark come from reposts and are unconfirmed by the company.

The revenue mix is also more concentrated: data center above 90%, with Vera Rubin already contributing nearly $20 billion as the new platform takes over. The gap between fundamentals and the share price is really a dispute over how long that concentration survives export controls and governance turmoil, not over last quarter's numbers.

## 3. Controls tighten on foundry work and overseas compute together

A new U.S. BIS rule published November 16 took effect November 19: for certain advanced logic chips made at 16/14nm or below, or with non-planar transistor architectures, foundries and OSATs must first presume the parts fall under 3A090.a; the previous exemption that let them prove "not controlled" by transistor count is gone; scrutiny of a customer's ultimate parent, actual control relationships and end use has also been strengthened.

A separate interim rule targets compute access: overseas data centers must obtain a license before providing large amounts of compute to Chinese companies, with a 90-day runway for those already operating; Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil also need licenses to import advanced AI chips.

The consequences have reached signed contracts. Moonshot AI cancelled a $150 million deal with an Indonesian cloud provider that was to rent a Blackwell Ultra B300 cluster to train Kimi K4; it had prepaid about 50%, far above the industry norm of 15%. TSMC and Samsung paused deliveries of some processors to Chinese customers pending licenses. Nvidia's interim CEO said publicly the company no longer sells chips to China.

The impact goes beyond AI accelerators. With the non-AI chip exemption removed, phone processor supply for Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo is also affected. Chinese domestic GPU stocks fell collectively that day — Tianshu Zhixin dropped more than 25% intraday, Enflame hit the limit down — while SMIC and Hua Hong rose against the trend, as the market pushed orders toward domestic foundries.

Rumour and rule need separating. The claim that Samsung had begun "look-through" reviews of Chinese chip companies was denied by Shanghai Securities News after checking with multiple parties in the supply chain; the rule's real force depends on how licenses are granted, and neither the share-price moves nor any single company's exposure has been independently verified.

## 4. Gemini 4 Neon: price position matters more than rank

Neon, released November 18, fills the middle of the Gemini family: a 1M-token context and multi-agent support. On Artificial Analysis it ranks third on the composite intelligence index at 61, takes first on the coding-agent index ahead of Claude Opus 5.5, and is priced at about 37.5% of the GPT-6.2 Astra and Claude Fable 5.2 flagships. API pricing is $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output.

Testing on the day after launch began to diverge. Jeremy Howard and Demis Hassabis reposted claims that it beats Fable 5.2 on most tasks; Riley Brown said it is good for coding and video editing but has a very obvious "it's not this, it's that" tic in prose and should not be used for writing; Xiangyang Qiaomu found Opus 5.5 still ahead on development efficiency. Several people called it a "small model"; the parameter count has not been disclosed.

The subscription gate became a talking point too: the $4.99 Google AI Plus tier cannot use Neon; the $19.99 Pro tier can. That explains why a "cheap" model triggered so much account switching, region changing and third-party client hunting after launch.

Google's developer relations team laid out the division of labour: Neon as the daily driver for coding and agents, Hydrogen at $1.50 input and $7.50 output per million tokens for basic tasks, chat and subagents, and Carbon for slower tests and long-horizon simulation. A price ladder plus capability tiers is really selling the right to route each task to a given model.

Evidence boundary: the rankings come from third-party evaluation, and the parameter count is community inference. If "small model" holds, it means distillation plus post-training now buys flagship-adjacent quality below 40% of flagship pricing, but no official specification confirms it yet.

Cutting price below 40% of the flagship changes defaults. Teams used to pick the most expensive model as the safe choice; with a mid-tier model leading a coding index, the token bill will move migrations earlier than evaluation scores do.

## 5. Google moves a world model and tool retrieval into the API

Genie 4 is live in the Gemini API and AI Studio: it generates 720p, 24fps worlds from text or images, steered live with WASD, prompts and 48kHz stereo audio, streamed over WebRTC (browser plus ephemeral tokens) or WebSocket. It is the first model on the new Live Interactions API, whose stated goal is to put standard calls and real-time, long-running sessions behind one interface.

Putting a 720p world model behind an API makes real-time controllability, not generation, the hard part: keyboard input, prompts and audio all arrive at once while frames stream back, which forces a low-latency bidirectional protocol. That is also why Google had to rework the API's session model at the same time.

The other release that day was a preview of Tool Retrieval. Agents load tool parameter schemas on demand instead of sending the entire tool library with every request, cutting input tokens and keeping selection accurate as tool counts grow. For teams with hundreds of tools, this matters more than swapping models.

There is a clear trade-off: the more tools, the more expensive it is to stuff every schema into context, and the more likely the model picks wrong from a long list; on-demand retrieval addresses both, at the cost of an extra retrieval call and its latency. It solves a scale problem, not a capability problem.

The Mac Gemini app, meanwhile, added computer use: tasks across folders, apps and the web, with an official demo turning a sketch into a rigged 3D model in Blender, while screen and cursor control stay with the user. All three point the same way — real-time environments, tool selection and local operation as API capabilities rather than product features.

These are vendor announcements. Genie 4's latency, concurrency ceiling and billing have not been tested by third parties, and Tool Retrieval's accuracy with very large tool libraries is still only the company's word.

## 6. OpenAI uses Codex to fix ChatGPT's performance regressions

OpenAI described its Performance Factory: Codex agents in the cloud automatically investigate and fix ChatGPT performance regressions. One Regression Workflow tracked more than 1,700 investigations over three months and located over 600 root causes, roughly 17 automated investigations a day.

This work used to mean watching dashboards, reading traces and reproducing by hand. Handing investigation and localization to agents moves people from finding problems to reviewing fixes, and the engineering gain comes mainly from investigation throughput rather than from any single fix being clever.

Regression Workflow is only one pipeline in the system; the Factory treats performance issues as production-line defects, with discovery, attribution and repair each owned by a stage rather than left to a manual check before release. That division of labour is more reusable than the specific numbers.

The boundary needs stating: this is OpenAI's own account, with no false-positive rate, miss rate, share of human review, or rollback rate after fixes ship. The article body was blocked by Cloudflare, so only the RSS summary and the morning digest's paraphrase can be checked; the 1,700 and 600 figures stand on the company's word for now.

## 7. Cline-Bench treats the evaluation itself as a target to defend

Cline's Cline-Bench consists of 47 coding tasks drawn from real user requests, each run three times, reporting pass@1, pass@3 and pass^3 together. The emphasis is less on task count than on anti-gaming: evaluation in a separate clean environment, protected tests restored, missing or skipped required tests counted as failures, and graders tested against deliberately tampered solutions. Task instructions and environments were also repaired by hand to preserve the original constraints.

The results form a narrow band. Gemini 4 Argon 51.1%, Opus 5.5 48.9%, Gemini 4 Carbon 48.9%, Sonnet 5.5 48.2%, GPT 6 Astra and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash tied at 44.7%, Kimi K3.1 Pro 43.3%, GLM 5.3 34.0%, Nemotron 3.5 Super VL 9.2%. On consistency, pass^3 puts Opus 5.5 highest at 42.6%, Carbon at 29.8% and DeepSeek at 21.3%.

The authors published 95% confidence intervals, mostly more than 20 percentage points wide — 38.3% to 63.8% for the top model — and said explicitly that the intervals do not support reading small gaps as capability differences. That caveat is worth more than the ranking: 47 tasks can separate usable from unusable, not several points between frontier models.

The board also records time and turns, which explains cost better than success rate. Gemini 4 Argon averaged 27.8 minutes and 80.5 turns; Sonnet 5.5 needed only 25.1 minutes and 49.7 turns; GPT 6 Astra 14.2 minutes and 59.9 turns; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 14.4 minutes but 120.3 turns. The same mid-40s success rate can come from thinking longer or from retrying more.

For buyers, the right use of such a board is to filter out models that clearly cannot do the work, then retest on your own repository. Using it to rank frontier models produces error bars bigger than the gaps.

## 8. Letting agents edit themselves, and edit other people's quantization recipes

Google's QUARD draws the boundary of self-improvement narrowly: the base model stays frozen, agents may edit only their own prompts, skills, tool rules and memory, and an edit is accepted only when its gains outweigh the behaviours it breaks. On 43 hosted SDK tasks it reached 80.80% accuracy, 8.89 points above the starting harness (p = 0.008), 6.28 points above Meta-Harness while using 54.5% fewer tokens per task; on procedural-memory tasks it led update correctness at 90.1% with zero memory pollution, while aggressive rewriters produced triple the pollution.

Its way of deciding what broke is worth copying: split each task into self-checks, grade trajectories against them each round, pin rules the agent already handles, and rank newly broken behaviours first. At test time a single frozen harness runs once, with no multi-round search.

MIT and Nvidia's QDA hands quantization recipe search to an agent: it scores candidates in minutes using KL divergence from the original model on reasoning traces it never sees, then gives promising candidates long generations to catch repetition loops. It beats the strongest public recipes by 0.9, 1.4 and 2.2 points at W4A16, W4A8 and W3A16, using 64 GPU-hours per search; on GLM-5.3-Flash at NVFP4 W4A4 it recovered 98.3% of FP8 accuracy against 96.9% for the vendor checkpoint, with no new inference kernel required.

Both turn tuning from human intuition into a verifiable search loop, using breakage detection or fast scoring to block regressions. The boundaries match too: both are papers and author summaries without independent replication, and QUARD's gains hold on 43 hosted tasks, not open-ended work.

## 9. Data center power is becoming a health bill for downwind states

An MIT team published in Environmental Research Letters, using hourly grid modelling to trace emissions from data center electricity: in 2023 that added air pollution caused roughly 650 premature deaths in the United States — 513 from PM2.5 and 137 from smog — about 5.3% of all power-related deaths nationally.

The key is spatial mismatch. Hourly grid modelling puts generation mix, transmission reach and data center load on the same time resolution, and finds that pollution and health damage often land in downwind states far from the facilities, while electricity revenue, taxes and jobs stay where the servers are. That adds a footnote to "data centers are local economic growth."

The 5.3% figure is worth remembering: it is not data centers' share of total emissions but the share of national power-related deaths attributed to their incremental electricity use. When discussing AI's energy cost, that is closer to the consequence than kilowatt-hours.

This is a grid-model estimate, not case-by-case attribution, and the conclusions are sensitive to generation mix, grid structure and the year studied.

## 10. Office agents are dropping the chat box

Microsoft's Today, announced at Ignite, is a clear signal. Product lead Nicolas Bustamante says it is not a chat box but a feed: overnight an agent works through email, calendar, Teams and documents, and in the morning the drafts, conflicts and documents awaiting review are already there. No prompting, nothing to configure; the person only sends, accepts or marks done. It starts inside Outlook and Copilot.

Several signals belong to the same batch. Devin Bot launched formally after honing with more than a thousand testers, able to plan projects, dispatch multiple Devins and keep validating changes, lifting the earlier one-task-at-a-time limit. Perplexity's Multiagent usage doubled in a day, concentrated in tasks that split into parallel pieces such as deck building, multi-source research, financial analysis and contact lists. Aravind Srinivas also said the Context API launched the day before added Decider checks after user feedback, screening for prompt injection before fetching.

The shared assumption: an agent's value is not in conversation turns but in finishing part of the work while you are away. That explains two things — why interaction is shifting from message streams to cards and lists, and why context, memory and permissions are becoming product differentiators faster than model scores.

Integration surface now decides adoption earlier than the model does. Grok Bot can read and write an Obsidian vault, LangChain's Design Agent lives inside Slack threads, another Grok Bot pattern watches Linear cards tagged as bugs, and Microsoft put Today inside Outlook and Copilot. The entry point is becoming the tool people already use, not a new chat window.

Boundary: Today starts only in Outlook and Copilot, and the Devin Bot and Multiagent growth figures are executives' own accounts without third-party usage data.

## Editorial conclusion

What actually changed today was how money is priced and how controls are enforced, not how many points another model gained. Anthropic traded the largest IPO on record for public markets and hundreds of billions in compute commitments; Nvidia fell to a 52-week low on its best quarter, which tells you the market is already valuing promises and delivery separately. On the technical side the evidence is still slow: work like Cline-Bench reminds us that a few points between frontier models cannot be resolved, and the automation gains Google and OpenAI announced are still their own accounts.
