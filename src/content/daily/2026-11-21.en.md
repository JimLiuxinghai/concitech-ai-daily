The most important change today is not another model release. It is that a formal proof of more than 17 million lines was publicly confirmed as correct by the mathematics community. Lean and Mathlib issued a joint statement on November 21 endorsing the Riemann Hypothesis proof produced by Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge with Claude. On the same day, OpenAI turned "preventing model capability distillation" into a measurable defense, the argument over AI valuations intensified after Anthropic's listing, and Nvidia dealt with a leadership vacuum and its exit from the Chinese market at once. No official blog source published anything new that day, so the signal came mainly from practitioners' tests and arguments on social platforms.

## Theme 1: The Riemann Hypothesis has a machine-verified conclusion, but it is not yet a theorem

The joint statement from the Lean and Mathlib communities is blunt: they confirm that the statement of the Riemann Hypothesis and its proof are both correct. It is backed by Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative. The object verified is more than 17 million lines of Lean code across over 170,000 modules; the community organized large-scale independent compilation, multiple independently implemented proof checkers passed, and the whole proof uses only 3 standard axioms with no extra assumptions slipped in.

The technical reason this matters is checkability. A formal proof is verified line by line by the kernel, so it cannot get by on "the code looks right," which makes its conclusion harder than any benchmark score. The same step also caps its reach: only in domains that can be formalized is this kind of verification final.

The boundaries are just as clear. The Clay Institute has not commented, there is no human-readable paper yet, and mathematicians have only begun interpreting the proof strategy. One widely repeated claim holds that expanding the proof into conventional prose would exceed 250,000 pages. The criticism from inside the field is well aimed: Lean passing is not the same as the Millennium Prize problem being officially declared solved, and definition alignment plus human review still sit in between.

The author's background is part of why this was taken seriously. Levent Alpöge is an Anthropic mathematician who previously worked with a team on the generalization of Hilbert's Tenth Problem across all number fields, a question posed in 1900. Because the proposer has that record, the community organized large-scale reproduction within a single day.

A second detail worth recording is the quality of the argument itself. Gary Marcus repeatedly stresses that he said in December 2024 that these techniques would perform best in closed, verifiable domains like mathematics and noticeably worse in open-ended real-world reasoning. François Fleuret points out that checking an AI proof with Lean and "checking" a human proof are not remotely the same act. Both are describing two sides of one thing.

## Theme 2: OpenAI treats "internal reasoning" as an asset that needs defending

OpenAI published "Combatting adversarial distillation" on how to block unauthorized extraction of model capability. What it defines as adversarial distillation is not a routine training method but behavior that violates terms, evades anti-abuse measures, and systematically collects outputs for training, with the focus on internal reasoning the model does not expose to users.

Why the reasoning trace is the target: a final answer carries only the conclusion, while full reasoning contains attempts, checks, and intermediate steps, which makes for richer training signal. OpenAI's internal experiments found that even when the teacher model was doing coding and defensive cybersecurity work, training a student on full reasoning traces could transfer stronger related capability than final answers, reasoning summaries, and tool-interaction records. That advantage varies with training method and reasoning budget.

The numbers come from one specific test setup. In adaptive black-box testing with six attack models, the average success rate for extracting reasoning from earlier requests fell from 54.0% for GPT-6 Astra with the original defenses to 0.2% for GPT-6.2 Astra with the new ones; extracting reasoning from the current request fell from 22.9% to 0.9%. Protection works at two layers. At the request layer, adversarial training hardens the model against reasoning extraction while preserving normal explanation, with input and output classifiers and delayed streaming as a second gate. The API binds protected reasoning to the original conversation history, so editing earlier messages discards later reasoning. At the activity layer, cross-account coordinated extraction is identified and leads are shared with other providers. The judgments about DeepSeek and Moonshot-related activity come from OpenAI's own investigation and attribution.

The easiest thing to misread is that 0.2%. It is the result of a specific reasoning-extraction test and cannot be generalized into "all distillation success rates dropped to 0.2%." The model and the defenses were updated together, so the entire gain cannot be credited to any single measure.

## Theme 3: The agent gap moved from the model to the loop's surroundings

The most useful engineering consensus of the day came from several unrelated posts. One developer noted that a free Google course and an open Stanford assignment make the same point: what determines whether an agent is reliable is the outer shell that handles braking and fallbacks. In that assignment's 88-point automated test, 48 points never call the model at all — they feed empty replies, malformed formats, and errors to test fault tolerance, with a budget cap that force-stops a runaway run. His example is telling: two AIs book a restaurant and both succeed, one checking the calendar before ordering, the other skipping the calendar and guessing a time that happens to be right. Judged only on outcome, both score full marks; ask either to book again tomorrow and it is obvious which one breaks.

Another write-up takes the loop apart more finely. The core flow is just a while loop — put the user message into history, call the model, execute the tool, append the result, call again — and what actually determines system quality is tool design, context management, and backend reliability. Tools have two requirements: the model must find it easy to fill in parameters, and returns must consume little context, with oversized results written to disk and a path returned instead. A sub-agent is essentially a tool call whose value is context isolation and clear division of labor. The engineering outside the loop is routinely underestimated: a run can last minutes, so the backend must handle streaming, heartbeats, and reconnection, cancellation and timeouts must propagate to the actual HTTP requests and subprocesses, and retries must account for idempotency.

Harrison Chase of LangChain offers the other side of the same experience: agents cannot reliably edit their own wiki, so the editing work belongs to a dedicated curator agent. That observation matches the conclusion above — what is missing is not a stronger model but a mechanism that keeps knowledge from rotting. Claude Code's new Code Mode heads the same way: instead of calling one tool and waiting, it writes a piece of JavaScript that runs several commands in parallel, filters output, and passes it on, while tests and builds can be pushed to the background for up to two hours.

None of this rests on a unified benchmark, but it comes from multiple independent practitioners and points to one actionable conclusion: hard-code the budget, the checks, and the error-return path first, and only then consider switching models.

## Theme 4: Inkling 2 Mini pushes the price-performance of local models to a new place

Thinking Machines' Inkling 2 Mini was the most discussed open model of the day. It uses a looped architecture in which 12 of its 24 layers run three times per token, with roughly 26B parameters. The design goal is explicit: trade repeated computation for parameter count, and squeeze the model onto a laptop.

Measured numbers come from several directions. Partner exolabs reports 210 tok/s decode and 3,620 tok/s prefill on a 96GB M5. Another developer reports it is 1.8x faster than Claude Sonnet 5.5 on an M5 Ultra Mac Studio and 11x cheaper at API pricing. Artificial Analysis places it first on efficiency and ninth on intelligence in the 78-point band. One evaluation puts cost per task at $0.59 against $3.53 for Qwen 3.8 27B.

The behavioral change deserves more attention: on science questions answered equally correctly, written reasoning dropped from about 3,703 tokens to 1,731, and "wait/hmm" style stalls fell from 9.7 per question to 3.6. Keeping the thinking inside the loop and writing less is good for cost and bad for anyone whose safety monitoring depends on reading the reasoning.

All of this is vendor and enthusiast framing with no independent third-party reproduction. What can be said is narrower: a 26B looped model reaches usable speed on consumer memory, and the baseline for local deployment has moved up a notch.

## Theme 5: After Anthropic's listing, the AI capital story splits in two

Anthropic has completed its IPO, and its official news page updated with listing-related entries on November 20. Dario Amodei's first public message was not about money; it was about his father. The detail was widely reshared and became a footnote to the day's mood.

The skeptical case centers on unit economics. Gary Marcus says the $2 trillion valuation did not hold, argues that with token prices falling Anthropic will struggle to approach profitability again, and relays criticism about retail buyers and passive index-fund purchases. Another observation in circulation is that hyperscaler capital expenditure far exceeds the revenue AI labs generate.

The rebuttal is equally specific. François Chollet lists several points: GPU spot prices are still rising and capacity is hard to secure; HBM and DRAM production is effectively sold out through 2029, with chip and memory supply expected to stay tight into the 2030s; frontier training compute is doubling roughly every five months and capability is scaling faster than before reasoning models arrived; inference margins run 70% to 85% with demand still growing geometrically.

Neither side convinced the other, and no new data can settle it immediately. The more useful move for a reader is not to pick a side but to name the disagreement: one looks at the gap between current revenue and valuation, the other at supply constraints and the compute demand curve. Both observations can hold at once.

## Theme 6: Nvidia handles a leadership vacuum and an exit from China at the same time

Nvidia reported third-quarter revenue of $115.844 billion and net profit of $65.3 billion, with data center revenue of $107.6 billion — more than 90% of the total. Its next-generation Rubin platform has begun shipping. Beyond the numbers, the company is still working through a leadership change: the interim CEO said publicly that the company will no longer sell chips to China, while Jensen Huang said he would be glad to return as CEO and the board continues to look for a permanent successor.

Policy tightened in parallel. The US Commerce Department issued an interim rule: overseas data centers that provide large amounts of compute to Chinese companies must first obtain a license, and operations already running get only a 90-day grace period; Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil also need licenses to import advanced AI chips. On governance, Nvidia's board set up a standing national security oversight committee chaired by independent director Suzanne Nora Johnson, with the national security and global trade compliance team reporting to both the committee and the interim CEO; the committee meets at least quarterly and brings in outside legal and trade experts.

Stacked together, these moves affect more than one company's income statement. The national boundaries of compute supply are being redrawn by administrative rules, and the rules take effect faster than capacity can be adjusted.

The boundary to keep: the exit-from-China statement comes from an industry daily's paraphrase, not from Nvidia's own announcement; the 90-day grace period and the five-country license list come from the same paraphrase, and the operative details await official text.

## Theme 8: DeepSeek pulls back free features while third parties fill the gap

On November 21 users noticed that opening a DeepSeek conversation someone else had shared now shows a notice: due to insufficient compute, shared conversations can no longer be continued. People who used the feature to write interactive stories and role-play would send a tuned persona to friends to carry on; now it can only be read. There was no official announcement, so whether this is a temporary shutdown or a permanent removal is unclear.

In the same period, DeepSeek hit a wave of one-star reviews in Chinese app stores, with someone even calling the 12345 government hotline to complain. Taken together, both point at one constraint: free long conversations burn the most compute, since every reply recomputes the preceding context, and when compute tightens the first things cut are features like this that do not earn money directly.

The third-party ecosystem is growing instead. A plugin marketplace for DeepSeek Harness announced more than 100,000 daily active users and added an in-conversation command to find and install plugins, plugin search, and "you may also like" recommendations. Its cost design is the interesting part: all of these AI features require the user to trigger and authorize them, so they never consume tokens by default and involve no remote model calls.

The value of this thread is not DeepSeek itself but a reusable pattern: when a large vendor withdraws free quota, demand does not disappear. It moves to lightweight tools willing to charge per use or per month.

## Theme 9: Four research results today all changed mechanisms rather than adding parameters

This cluster deserves to be read together because all of it addresses how training and inference are organized, not how to make a model bigger.

One 27B model tried to diagnose and modify its own weights without gradients and without a full training run; after self-editing, accuracy on unseen problems rose 6.6 points, and a corrupted model recovered from 7% to 70% across six vision benchmarks. The point is not the score but that "can a model be its own engineer" became an experimentable question.

Colombo does autonomous research: it organizes conjectures, does mathematical work, and replans against evidence, and is said to have reached optimal or near-optimal results on more than fifty optimization settings, with adversarial co-ascent and Lean formal certification built in. EvoX takes another route, using a persistent recursive software world in which later generations inherit code, contracts, and tests; across 1,019 agent sessions and 123.4 hours it wrote a C compiler in 219,700 lines of Rust, raising the number of passing fixed tests from 61 to 93.

One more piece is more theoretical: zero-temperature attention keeps only the best witness term and its tied count, passing only the bits needed for comparison, and stays near-perfect on retrieval sets a thousand times larger than the training scale. All four are single-source paper summaries with no independent reproduction, and the specific figures should be read as the authors' own framing.

## Editorial conclusion

What these stories share is not that "AI got stronger again" but that several things entered a checkable phase at once: a mathematical proof was verified line by line by a kernel, distillation defenses got a measurable success rate, local model throughput got reproducible numbers, and the capital story was forced to lay out both bullish and bearish assumptions. The parts that can be verified are moving fast; the parts that cannot are still arguing in place.
