The most concrete item today comes from MIT CSAIL, which had GPT-6 Astra read and edit another model's weights: changing roughly 0.016% of Qwen3.5-0.8B's parameters lifted MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1%. On the same day, OpenAI published Pando, a Git backend rewritten for Codex-era concurrency, and Anthropic updated its IPO filing, putting $895.4 billion in compute commitments on the record. Capability is moving downward into the stack while the bills grow, and neither story has independent verification yet.

## One: Weight-space programming — models start reading and writing other models' parameters

Four MIT CSAIL researchers published a set of experiments on November 2: GPT-6 Astra was given access to another model's raw weights and asked to read, locate, and modify parameters against natural-language goals, with no gradients and no training loop. They call it weight-space programming.

The clearest case is Qwen3.5-0.8B. With thinking mode enabled, the model tends to fall into repetitive loops, and 93% of its answers ran to the length limit. Astra worked on the weights inside a Python sandbox and ran 50 forward passes on practice problems, locating a direction written by the MLP layers at layers 15 and 19 and subtracting it from each of 64 output columns — 131,072 weights in total, about 0.016% of the model. Afterwards MATH-500 went from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%, and IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%.

Other experiments probe the boundary between reading and writing. Given four structurally identical PPO policy networks of 17,253 weights each, with no game names, Astra identified Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, and Freeway from the weight text alone. A simulated uphill car that had trained for a billion steps and failed all 256 attempts became a case for one reply: shown the weights and a single failure recording, Astra changed about 3.5% of the parameters and the car then succeeded in all 1,024 runs. On three MinAtar games it wrote weights in one shot with no data, no gradients, and no environment rollouts, beating a PPO trained for 500 million steps on Breakout. A 9×9 Go network with about 1.48 million parameters would not fit in context, so Astra read and wrote tensors through a sandbox and, after 50 forward passes, beat a same-sized PPO trained on roughly 20 million games.

The boundaries matter. Every result here is self-reported by the authors and has not been independently reproduced. The same method gave no improvement on Gemma 3 270M, a model that rarely loops; the authors' own conclusion is that weight editing removes obstacles blocking existing capability but cannot add capability the model never had. In a similar experiment published separately, one task was found to have fitted on test-set data and that result was voided. Jürgen Schmidhuber, for his part, said publicly that this is the same idea as the fast weight programmer work of the 1990s — an old approach rediscovered. It is a demonstration of direction, not yet a reusable engineering technique.

## Two: Pando — rebuilding Git for agent-driven writes

OpenAI published a technical post the same day explaining Pando, a Git-compatible backend rewritten for its own monorepo. After Codex pushed the repository into heavy parallel branch writes and massive concurrent reads, the old system began to slow down, fail merges, and at one point dropped below 90% reliability.

The team did not abandon the Git workflow; they replaced the backend. They relaxed two old assumptions: individual refs stay serializable while the repository as a whole only guarantees causal consistency, and packs move into object storage with metadata recording only tips, dependencies, and pack pointers. Pushes to different branches no longer block each other, and readers of multiple branches no longer have to assume a single snapshot.

On results, production median push latency fell to about one fifth of the previous figure, with daily throughput approaching 1 PB of Git data. Median packs are under 20 KiB and P99 under 3 MiB. On the read side, have/want requests map to existing ref versions and cacheable packs are served through packfile-uri, with a hit rate above 99%, enough for a standard machine to saturate 10 Gbps.

The project went from kickoff to production in about 10 weeks, was rewritten in Rust, and rolled out through a dual-write proxy before staged cutover. Greg Brockman said the company is considering offering Pando to others. These figures come from the official post and the author's summary; simulation numbers and production numbers should be read separately.

## Three: Anthropic's twelvefold growth and $895.4 billion in commitments

Anthropic updated its S-1/A, disclosing third-quarter results for the first time: revenue of nearly $17 billion, about twelve times the prior year; operating costs of $17.5 billion; and a quarterly net loss of $23.3 billion, up from $12.1 billion a year earlier.

The more revealing figure is the commitment structure. Total compute and infrastructure contracts come to about $895.4 billion, of which $447.5 billion must be paid before 2031, and only $213.8 billion is cancellable or conditional. Revenue is rising, but the spending schedule now extends five years out.

The same disclosure produced two opposite readings. One holds that twelvefold growth shows real enterprise demand. Gary Marcus argued that the real genius lies in making people believe these numbers add up to a world-class business when the company is teetering on fiscal disaster. Both judgments rest on the same document; the disagreement is about how likely those commitments are to be honored.

Keep the boundary in view: these figures come from media summaries and secondhand roundups of the IPO filing, not from audited statements. The cancellable share suggests most commitments are firm, but how firm depends on contract terms that the public materials do not disclose line by line.

## Four: Claude API message threads — dropping the history resend

Anthropic has opened a beta of message threads for the Messages API. The server holds the conversation for a few minutes, and the next turn only needs to send the new message plus the ID of the previous reply, instead of resending the full history.

The official numbers: for requests of 500,000 to 1 million tokens, median time from request to first output falls by about 35%, and median request size in long conversations drops from roughly 800 KB to 1.8 KB. Price, rate limits, and token consumption are unchanged — what is saved is upload bandwidth, not billing.

There are real limits. A thread expires after five minutes without a new request and returns 404; the system prompt, tools, and model cannot change mid-thread; and it does not support zero data retention or HIPAA compliance, which makes it harder for enterprises with strict retention rules. Claude Code already enables the feature by default for most users.

The value of the change is that it admits an awkward premise: an agent session can fire hundreds of requests, and resending long history is itself a source of latency. Threads remove that repeated upload; cache breakpoints are still something you set yourself.

## Five: DeepSeek's resident router — moving memory out of the session

The DeepSeek Harness team released dsh-superagent, an MIT-licensed prototype aimed at a single GitHub repository: a resident Router accepts and dispatches work, each ticket goes to a context-isolated Worker session running in parallel, and finished work arrives as a pull request for human review.

It targets the problem of agents being tied to one manual session. According to the project description, this structure buys cross-session memory, concurrent tasks, and asynchronous interaction; new requests can arrive while background work is still running. The team also shipped a creator mode that lets users write their own plugins.

The repository's self-reported simulation shows a p95 completion time of 15.7 minutes against a 29.5-minute single-session baseline. Discount that figure: the data comes from synthetic messages and simulated tools, the authors note the experiment duration was scaled down by a factor of four, and there is no third-party reproduction.

What is worth remembering is not the 15.7 minutes but the division of labor: "remember what was discussed earlier" is lifted out of the model's context and handed to routing and session management. That is the same direction as the context-transport changes Codex and Claude Code made the same day.

## Six: Decision models become their own layer — retrieval, hearing, extraction

DecisionRAG from Cornell turns retrieval into decisions made on a tree: the corpus already carries a topic-document-passage hierarchy, and the decision model Jev makes one forward pass per level over all candidates, judging whether a branch is worth expanding, without generating tokens and without a prebuilt vector index.

The authors report that against dense retrieval plus an 8B reranker, R@1 on MultiDoc2Dial is 13.8 points higher, and Hit@1 on Natural Questions over 2.68 million passages is 17.4 points higher; a single pass takes 0.7 to 1.8 seconds and costs $0.002 to $0.023. Using GPT-5 to generate navigation decisions on the same tree with the same budget produces similar accuracy but runs 34 to 145 times slower and costs 21 to 25 times more.

Two other components point the same way. Hume's audio decision API listens to the audio itself and answers questions such as whether a caller is frustrated, with confidence scores, without transcription. Sage 2 does text extraction in a single forward pass, returning structured values, source spans, and probabilities, with the vendor claiming document extraction at 75 times lower cost than Opus 5.5.

The common thread is that models no longer complete a task by generating prose; they emit a judgment or a structure. The caveats are also common: DecisionRAG's accuracy depends on tree structure and node descriptions, and pruning is irreversible; the vendor latency and price comparisons are self-reported.

## Seven: Code Mode — the tool catalog collapses into one exec

Philipp Schmid documented a convergence in tool calling: instead of returning one JSON action per turn, the agent receives a single exec(code) and uses TypeScript or Python in a sandbox to chain queries, filters, edits, and parallel calls in one pass, with most intermediate results never entering context.

What makes this viable is a change in model capability: frontier models are steadier at composing dependent actions within a single generation, and MCP servers increasingly expose both inputSchema and outputSchema, so the model knows return types before running anything. The harness compresses dozens of tool schemas into one type declaration, and text(), image(), and audio() decide which outputs carry into the next turn.

There is already product data: after Factory moved Droid 1.0 to Code Mode, Terminal-Bench 4 accuracy rose about 3.5 points and model spend fell about 15%. Pi and OpenAI Codex already default to or support the approach.

The author leaves room for doubt: not every agent will truly end up with one tool, and many CLIs and Skills optimized for bash and human-readable text may not adapt cleanly to typed execution. This looks more like a re-layering of the tool stack than an endpoint.

## Eight: OpenAI moves safety judgment inside the model

OpenAI disclosed the activation classifiers it has deployed on internal and external traffic for its strongest models since GPT-5.6: internal activations are scored per token in real time, allowing a violating response or tool call to be blocked mid-generation and passed to a reasoning model for review.

The company says these probes are more robust than larger text classifiers against the jailbreaks it tested, that accuracy improves as models get stronger, and that they transfer well across post-training checkpoints.

The limits are equally clear: a new base model requires retraining, and the probes can only catch violation types that can be explicitly trained for. Safety judgment moves inside the model, but its coverage is still drawn by the training data.

OpenAI also raised its security bug bounty the same day, adding bonuses for report quality, researcher loyalty, and priority campaigns, and announced a next internal security sprint with Cathedral Systems to test and harden its defenses using AI. The first turns sustained outside research into a priced relationship; the second admits the attack surface has grown beyond what internal teams can cover alone.

## Nine: World models and spatial understanding — three research lines in one day

Tsinghua's Andrew Yao and Yang Yuan team proposed the Algebraic World Model, which generates Python hypotheses from environment exploration and then condenses the rules into Lean 4 assertions, turning a world model from a record of phenomena into something machine-checkable. Reports say a system built on Opus 5 cleared all 183 levels of ARC-AGI-3 with a score of 99.9% and average action counts at 43% of the human baseline.

The other side of the same direction is evaluation. SpatialScout asks an agent to choose its own viewpoints, adjust the camera and geometry tools itself, and finally output 3D object coordinates; the formal benchmark covers 80 scenes and 3,140 instances, where Opus 5.5 averages 41.71% IoU, Astra 33.16%, and Sonnet 5.5 only 32.28%. Even the strongest models reach only about four tenths on active exploration.

Long context saw progress at the mechanism level. A team from Fudan and Shanghai Innovation Institute's OpenMOSS pulled apart NoPE full attention alongside sliding-window and gated linear attention layer by layer and proposed changes such as SWLA; on RULER NIAH-SK1, a model trained at 4k extrapolates to 64k without training and still scores 100%. The code has been released.

All three lines set checkable thresholds for how models understand the world rather than competing on narrative. The boundaries are also consistent: AWM's proofs hold only for the observed world, so anything exploration missed remains a local truth; SpatialScout's scores show active spatial understanding is still early; and the OpenMOSS result is extrapolation on a single benchmark, not real long-document tasks.

## Ten: The power and the money — interconnection, financing, and local resistance tighten together

OpenAI introduced the Grid Protectors program, working with utilities, regulators, and policymakers so that future compute campuses can reduce demand when the grid is strained, starting with the Effingham County campus in Georgia. Per the official description, the aim is to turn data centers from a large customer that simply takes power into a dispatchable load, while protecting consumer electricity costs.

The archived summary notes that the original piece gave specific reducible capacity and electricity cost figures for the Georgia Power contract, but those numbers were not preserved here, so they are not cited. That means the program can currently be judged by direction, not by capacity.

The money side is a different structure. Tencent Tech walked through infrastructure financing for CoreWeave, Oracle, Lambda, and Anthropic: as bonds and equities move, the market starts distinguishing collateral structures and debt service capacity. One $63 billion arrangement would use an SPV to buy Broadcom AI accelerators and networking gear and lease them to Anthropic, with Broadcom guaranteeing residual equipment value.

Such deals package equipment, leases, and credit into layers so that capital with different risk appetites takes a slice each. That supports very large procurement, at the cost of repricing across every layer if rents or valuation expectations shift.

The third pressure point is local. Reporting from a Brooklyn election event found AI and data centers running through nearly every discussion; the piece cited Data Center Watch figures that nearly $200 billion of data center projects have been delayed, stalled, or blocked by political and community opposition.

Read together, the picture is complete: purchase contracts that must be honored for years, local permits that are getting harder to obtain, and power and interconnection terms that have to be negotiated. Figures and state-level progress come from third-party material cited in the reporting and were not checked item by item.

## Editorial conclusion

Several of today's threads point at the same shift: models are being allowed to operate directly on things that training pipelines and infrastructure used to own — weights, context transport, the write path into source control. Capability is moving downward while the business side tightens: Anthropic's commitments run to 2031, and OpenAI's new round is only a third of its target. Technical progress and financial constraints are now moving at different speeds.
